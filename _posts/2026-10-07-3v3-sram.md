# SRAMs on Open Source Silicon

In November 2022, three months after the GF180MCU PDK was made open source, someone on the
[open source silicon forum posted a complaint](https://web.open-source-silicon.dev/t/5096040/holy-cow-these-gf180-sram-are-huge-compared-to-the-sky130-on):

> Holy cow, these gf180 SRAM are huge compared to the sky130 ones.

They weren't kidding: GF180MCU's 256x8 SRAM was ~4x bigger than SKY130's 256x32 and manages to store less. It's no good for
people with limited silicon area to build in.

Tim Edwards -- the writer of Magic and Netgen, core components of the modern open source ASIC flow -- replied:

> Note that this is a 5V SRAM. All of our IP for GF180MCU is for 5V operation.

And that was it... for a while at least. Luckily, there's been some advancements recently. So let's skip a few years
(and a defunct company) ahead to present day and see what has changed.

## Existing Options
The only SRAMs available for the GF180MCU process were Global Foundries' own 5 V macros - they were large and severely
impeded the space you had left for your project, but what else could you do? After the shutting down of eFabless and
with Google stopping their GFMPW runs, there was no incentive to improve them - why bother if they wouldn't get taped
out? Now with [wafer.space](https://wafer.space), that changes -- GF180MCU is back on the table.

Edwards knew that the GF180MCU PDK desperately needed a 3.3 V library, but with [Tholin already working on a cell library]({{"/news/a-need-for-speed-tholins-own-standard-cell-library" | relative_url }}),
he turned his attention back to the very thing diagnosed back in 2022.

## Shrinking It Down
Set on creating a 3.3 V capable SRAM, Edwards began by looking at the 2 KiB (256 word) macro for arbitrary reasons. He describes his
approach as a "hack", but it's a pretty clever approach to reusing components that were available:

1. Design a core cell for the 3.3 V devices based on the GF-provided SRAM rules (that are publicly available)
2. Determine the size difference between a 3.3 V core cell to the existing 5 V core cell
3. Import the 5 V SRAM with the scaling determined by the previous step - the X and Y scales were different, making it slightly tricky
4. Re-import the individual devices scaled by the width/length difference between minimum-length 5 V devices and minimum-length 3.3 V devices
5. Clean up the resulting mess

After a week of hard work, he had his first layout ready, and the process nailed down for porting the rest of the macros.
For the others, he manipulated the height of the block and added in extra rows where necessary. Original netlists were
converted using a quick Python script and cleaned up by hand. He even created XSchem schematics which had been missing
in the PDK, making it a very valuable addition to the library.

Edwards compared the two devices together, saying that his 3.3 V SRAM takes up half the area of the 5 V SRAM --
wonderful news to anyone who was wanting to include an SRAM block in their design. He goes further to say that "it [the
5 V SRAM] makes the process more like a 0.5µm process than a 0.18µm process. The 3.3 V devices are also large but makes
it more like a 0.25µm process.".

### An Extra Bit?
During his exploration of the foundry-provided SRAM, he noticed that the decoder circuitry went up to 10 bits, yet
Global Foundries only ever utilized 9 bits -- their largest macro maxed out at a depth of 512 words, or 4 KiB.

There isn't a clear reason as to why they didn't provide a 8 KiB macro -- perhaps it didn't meet their requirements, or
maybe they just put in the extra bit because they could? It's all speculation, but the most important part is that
Edwards took the liberty of creating the 8 KiB macro that had originally been missing.

Unfortunately, for any larger sizes, considerable work would have to be done on the decoder circuitry as his "hack"
method would no longer work.

### Testing & Results
These shrunk down SRAMs are expected to be faster because they're using thinner oxide devices, despite the fact that
it's using the same SRAM core cells as the 5 V macros.

An initial version was taped out on Run #1, and some preliminary testing was done just in time to tell everyone that his
work was functional and operating to at least 100 MHz before Run #2 closed.

Unfortunately due to some delivery snags, Edwards wasn't able to get his hands on the extra logic analyzer he would've
needed to complete an exhaustive test of the SRAMs, but his testing was good enough to call the part functional.

Characterization still remains to be done, but his work has advanced the GF180MCU PDK and provided the community with
memory that they can use to build their projects with.

## Memory Generators
A different approach to getting memory on silicon is by using a generator. Thanks to the regular structure of a memory
component, macros can be built algorithmically and without tedious hours of manual work.

A benefit of having a memory generator available is that you, as the user, can decide precisely how large you want the
memory block to be. This allows you to optimize your area usage without wasting precious silicon on memory that sits
unused.

Staf Verhaegen, of Chips4Makers, ported his [flexmem SRAM generator](https://gitlab.com/Chips4Makers/c4m-flexmem) to use
the GF180MCU PDK and has taped out a handful of test chips on Run #2. They're still in production, so there's no test data
or characterization yet.
